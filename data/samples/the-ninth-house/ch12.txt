Chapter 12: The Salt Tithe

The breakthrough, when it came, was smaller and duller than Sorrel had
imagined it would be. She had been cross-referencing the ledger's stray
numbers against the Salt Tithe rolls — the ancient, unglamorous tax the crown
levied on the Quarter's salt trade, paid in coin or in labor, recorded every
quarter since before anyone could say why — and found that a column she had
taken for dates was not dates at all. It was a tithe-roll reference format,
three centuries out of use, the kind only a Chancery archivist or a very old
institution would still think to write in.

"Someone wrote this ledger like they learned bookkeeping before the reign
before last," she told Denna, who looked unimpressed until Sorrel explained
what that meant, and then looked considerably less unimpressed.

"That's not a court cipher. That's an institutional one."

"That's what I thought too."

A boy she didn't recognize — thin, watchful, perhaps thirteen — appeared at her
elbow with the particular confidence of someone who had been sent rather than
wandered. This was Oswin, though Sorrel would not learn his name for weeks yet.

"Message for the chancery girl," he said, and pressed a folded, unsealed note
into her hand before she could ask who from, then vanished into the Chancery's
afternoon crowd as neatly as he'd arrived.

The note held only one line: *You're asking the right questions. Ask them
somewhere safer.*

Sorrel read it twice, folded it into her sleeve, and did not, in fact, ask
anywhere safer at all.
