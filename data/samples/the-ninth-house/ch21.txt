Chapter 21: Partial Answers

"Why does it matter to you," Sorrel asked, once she had found her voice again,
"what my grandmother's grandmother was called? The Ninth House fell before any
of that meant anything to anyone but historians."

"It matters because the Ninth House didn't only keep names," Thessaly said.
"It kept a particular kind of knowledge, and knowledge like that prefers to
stay close to blood that remembers it, even when the blood itself doesn't
know why. You've unpicked two ciphers no living Choir member could unpick
faster than you did. That isn't chancery training, Sorrel. That's something
older, surfacing."

"That's not an answer. That's a very polite way of not answering."

"It's the only honest answer I have today," Thessaly said, and for once did
not soften the refusal with anything further. "The rest, you'll have to
survive long enough to ask again."

Sorrel left the Undercroft more unsettled than she had entered it, which she
would not have believed possible that morning.

At the Chancery, Ser Robart Kell noticed what Master Vane had not: that the
scribe with the troublesome ledger had missed two full afternoons in the past
fortnight, always vaguely accounted for, never quite convincingly. He said
nothing to Vane. He said nothing to Sorrel either, not yet — only began,
quietly, keeping his own private ledger of her comings and goings, the way a
guard-captain learns to keep track of the one loose thread in an otherwise
orderly household, long before he decides what, if anything, to pull.
